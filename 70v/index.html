Assumptions and limits

  • All the load is treated as sitting at the far end of the cable. On a real line the speakers are spread along the run, so the cable carries less current further out and the true loss is lower. This is a deliberate worst-case simplification.
  • The amplifier is treated as holding its full rated line voltage at its terminals, and the speaker load as a plain resistance equal to V² ÷ P. Real transformer-coupled speakers vary with frequency, so treat the results as estimates.
  • Transformer insertion loss is the power the speaker transformers use up themselves, added on top of the tap total. 1 dB is a common allowance. Use the figure on the speaker datasheet if it gives one, or 0 if the maker states its tap ratings already include it.
  • Cable resistance is DC resistance of copper only. Cable inductance and capacitance are ignored; they matter little at these lengths and audio frequencies on a high-impedance line.
  • Wire resistance values are nominal, at 20 °C. AWG sizes: solid annealed copper, from NBS Handbook 100, Copper Wire Tables (Table 5, values as printed). mm² sizes: maximum DC resistance for Class 2 stranded plain copper conductors, from IEC 60228, Conductors of insulated cables. Stranded AWG, tinned and flexible cables run a few percent higher: pick "Custom" and type the figure from the cable datasheet when it matters. Other temperatures are scaled with the copper coefficient 0.00393 per °C.
  • Local electrical code applies and is not checked here. For example, in the United States the NEC Class 2 and Class 3 wiring rules limit what cable and installation methods may be used on a given amplifier output. Check with the amplifier maker's listing and the authority having jurisdiction.
